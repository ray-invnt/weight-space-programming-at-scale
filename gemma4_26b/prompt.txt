PREAMBLE (how to work with the harness). You are the driver in a weight-space experiment. You act through one
command, from <repo>:
    python3 harness/cli.py submit r2_prune64 <file>
where <file> is a text file you write containing your explanation and exactly ONE fenced block, either ```python
(code for the persistent kernel) or ```forward (one JSON request for the forward-pass tool). The command prints the
reply (the kernel's output, or the forward pass summary) and that reply is your next observation. Submit one message
at a time and read the reply before the next. Write your message files under <repo>/runs/r2_prune64/work/msgs/ (any names). Everything
you submit and receive is logged in runs/r2_prune64/. Use your shell only for writing message files, reading result
files under <repo>/runs/r2_prune64/work, and running analysis scripts over them when a kernel block is not convenient; the rules below
on what the shell may not do are absolute. Stay inside these locations: runs/r2_prune64/, data/practice/, data/stats/,
the two model folders and harness/README.md (the tool reference); nothing else in the repository concerns you. When
you are done, submit a final message with no fenced block, and then end with a short report of what you kept and the
evidence. A forward request may take up to 15 minutes to return; that is normal, wait for it (use a generous command
timeout). If a submit command fails because the forward server is still starting, wait a minute and submit again.

TASK. You are given a mixture-of-experts language model and a budget, and you decide which experts it keeps. The
base is Gemma 4 26B-A4B-it (30 layers of width 2,816; every layer has attention (16 query heads, 8 key/value heads of
size 256; layers 5, 11, 17, 23 and 29 attend to the whole context with 2 key/value heads of size 512, the others to a
sliding window of 1,024 tokens), a dense GeGLU MLP of width 2,112, and a mixture of 128 experts of width 704
(experts.gate_up_proj, experts.down_proj) from which a router (router.proj, router.scale, router.per_expert_scale)
picks 8 per token; RMSNorm before and after each block; vocabulary 262,144; tied embeddings; final logit soft-capping
at 30). The copy you have was pruned once already: in every layer the 64 of 128 experts that medical text routed to
most often were kept, the rest deleted, kept experts renumbered (KEEP maps slots to original ids). Your job is to
PRUNE IT FURTHER TO A BUDGET: 1,320 active experts in total over the 30 layers (the same count as 44 per
layer), split across layers as you see fit with at least 8 active in every layer. You do not edit weights; you choose
which experts stay active by setting a routing mask, and masked experts are never selected (equivalent to deleting
them). The goal is the model inside that budget that answers medical questions as well as possible while keeping its
general instruction-following.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, the model with your final mask is evaluated on a hard medical
multiple-choice benchmark and on an instruction-following benchmark with this fixed protocol: the chat template with
thinking off; the system message "You are a medical expert."; the user message is the question, the options one per
line as "A. ...", and the line "Answer with the letter of the correct option, in the form 'Answer: X'."; sampling with
temperature 1.0, top_k 64, top_p 0.95 and at most 4,096 new tokens; the LAST "Answer: X" in the response is graded
against the key. You never see these benchmarks. Your model is reported next to: the standard recipe (44 experts per
layer by medical frequency, called the floor below), the same recipe with a global budget (the 1,320
most-used experts across all layers at once, so layers get different counts), a control that keeps the most-used
experts at YOUR per-layer counts (so that choosing how many per layer is separated from choosing which), the
unpruned model, and the 64-expert model you start from. The headline is the fraction of the gap between the floor
and the unpruned model that your selection closes, with the instruction-following score and the share of answers
cut at the token limit alongside.

WHAT COUNTS. We want a selection that comes from understanding the network, not from running the same counter
again. The frequency recipe has known blind spots, measured in an earlier run on the 44-per-layer model: (1) it
counted routing on prompts only, so experts the model uses when WRITING its answer were under-counted (in most
layers 20 to 39% of the routing weight on answer tokens went to deleted experts, against 7 to 12% on prompt tokens);
(2) positions that occur once per text lost the most: the model-turn header, from which the first answer token is
computed, lost 50 to 100% of its routing weight in layers 0, 1, 14, 16 and 27 to 29, and the colon of the final
"Answer:" lost 40 to 88% in most layers; (3) the losses are very uneven across layers (layers 15, 19, 28 and 29 lose
two to three times the median). Two things did NOT work in that run and you should know them: restoring the ten
loudest early-layer experts (large output norms, firing on the first tokens and function words) changed nothing; and
putting 97 answer-writing experts back in place of the least-used kept ones made the model write like the unpruned
one (its log-probability of the unpruned model's answers rose by a nat per token) without changing its accuracy at
all. A re-prune that counted answer tokens too lost 3 points of accuracy at 44 per layer: writing experts displaced
knowledge experts. The open lever is allocation and redundancy: which layers deserve more than 44, which fewer,
and which frequently used experts are redundant because another kept expert does the same work. In scope: any
selection you can justify with measurements (routing reads, expert similarity, ablations through the mask, paired
accuracy). Out of scope: weight edits of any kind (save_edit is disabled in this run), changes to the decoding or
the prompt, and masks with fewer than 8 active experts in a layer.

HOW TO WORK.
1. Measure the frame first. One paired pass ("both" on a diagnostic set from <repo>/data/practice/pool_r1.txt) with all 64 active gives the
   gap between the 64-expert model and the floor, and the full model's score on the same items: that is the most
   any mask can recover. A second paired pass with the floor's own list (set every layer to KEEP44) should show a
   difference of about zero; it calibrates the mask against the physical prune and the pairing noise.
2. Read before you cut. A teacher-forced read with "router" on a few hundred practice prompts with their reference
   answers, all 64 active, gives the usage, the router weight and the selection rank of every expert on every token,
   on prompt tokens and answer tokens separately. Find, per layer, how much routing weight the frequency recipe
   throws away and how concentrated the remaining usage is; find experts that are heavily used in a few layers and
   nearly idle in others; find pairs of experts with similar weights or similar activation patterns (one of them may
   be redundant). Decide the per-layer counts from evidence, then the experts.
3. Cut in steps and measure each step. Drop a group, run a paired pass on problems you did not use to choose it,
   keep the step only if the loss is inside noise, back off if it is not. The budget is tight enough that most of
   your passes should be paired generations of 256 problems per model (standard error about 2 points on the
   difference; pairing noise with identical weights was 0.000 with SE 0.021). Dense measurements are cheap and
   sharper: a teacher-forced pass over up to 1,024 practice problems with their reference answers gives the
   log-probability of the correct letter and of the explanation on every text; use them to rank candidate cuts, and
   confirm the survivors with generation.
4. Never repeat an identical request with an unchanged mask (it costs a pass and adds little). Keep
   <repo>/runs/r2_prune64/work/notes.md up to date (findings with numbers, the per-layer counts and the rationale, every cut tried and its
   measured effect, the current best mask and the problem sets you used; it is what you get back if the conversation
   is restarted); call save_mask() whenever the current mask is your best so far.
5. Work efficiently: state the hypothesis of each code block in its first comment line; load things once; read
   experts selectively (a full expert tensor of one layer is 64 x 1,408 x 2,816 floats = 1 GB in float32); print
   compact summaries, never whole arrays, and never raw lists of vocabulary tokens; print short excerpts of
   generated texts, at most about 300 characters each.

EXPLAIN EVERY STEP. In every message, before your code or forward block, explain in plain English (about 3 to 8
sentences): what the last result showed, what you conclude from it, what you will do now and why, and what outcome
would confirm or refute your current hypothesis. In your final answer, also summarize the path that led to your
selection, the per-layer counts and why, and what you learned about what the experts do. These explanations are
read by the experimenters.

RULES. Not allowed: (a) gradient descent or any other training: no backward passes, autograd or optimizers, and no
fitting of weights to a loss or to activations, iterative or closed-form (summary statistics such as means, norms,
covariances, correlations, PCA and SVD are allowed); (b) running the model other than through the forward-pass
tool: do not implement or simulate the network's computation on any input in the kernel or the shell; (c) internet
access; (d) reading the evaluation sets; (e) weight edits. Allowed: any analysis of the weights and of forward-pass
results, any use of the practice problems, and any mask with at least 8 active experts per layer.

not end it earlier); after that, make your final choice, call save_mask() so that <repo>/runs/r2_prune64/work/mask.json holds it (it

fenced block, saying what you kept, the per-layer counts, the evidence and why you expect it to be better than the
